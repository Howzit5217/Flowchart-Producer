# Flowchart-Producer
Paste pseudocode, get a flowchart — style it (colors, fonts, sizes, bold, highlighting), run it and watch what it holds, send it as a link, export it as SVG, PNG or code. No install needed — but if you want it as an app of its own, install it straight from the browser (Settings → Install as an app, or Share → Add to Home Screen on an iPhone). No app store, and it works offline.

Three ways in, one tab each:
- **Pseudocode** — write it, or just describe the program in plain English ("Ask the user for their age. If they are 18 or older, tell them they can vote."). A description is read into proper pseudocode, shown beside what you wrote (side by side in full screen), and drawn — your own words are never replaced.
- **Flowchart** — draw the chart yourself, shape by shape, and read it back as pseudocode.
- **Code** — write or paste the program in Python, Java, C#, C++ or JavaScript — or TypeScript, C, Kotlin, Swift, Go or Rust (the language is recognized by itself), in one file or several; it is read into pseudocode and drawn — lists, dictionaries and classes included — and you can run it, open it full screen, or translate it into Python, Java, C#, C++ or JavaScript. When something is wrong and the page can tell what (a missing `;`, bracket, `}` or quote, one too many, a line not lined up, a misspelled name like `pritn` or `Sytem`), **Build the chart** puts it right before drawing, with a progress bar while it works, and a **Fixed N mistakes** button under the code lists each mistake and what was done, so you can learn from them (Ctrl+Z, or **Put it back as it was**, undoes it); full screen has a **Check** button that does the same without drawing.

Every yellow warning and red error comes with a way to put it right. Where the page knows the answer it is a button; where it needs something only you know — the value a name starts at, the test a loop stops on, the line an If ends after, the words for a shape or where an arrow goes — a small box under the warning asks for it, and the page writes your answer in the right place, draws the chart again and, for a run that stopped, runs it again.

**Games** in the top bar has fourteen games to play, each a real program you can read as a flowchart while you play it: nine quick ones (Coin Toss, Higher or Lower, Twenty-One Sticks, Dice Duel, Quick Math, Pig, Hangman, Code Breaker, Lunar Lander) and five big ones (Dungeon Escape, Connect Four, Blackjack, Battleship, Minesweeper). **Play** on the game's card runs it all at once and puts your pace back afterwards; **Run** goes at the pace you chose, to watch the game work step by step. Every game is written in modules, and the **Charts** switch shows it either way: **Multiple** (a chart for each module beside the main one) or **One flowchart** (every module drawn where it is called). That is the same setting as **Options → Modules and functions in one chart**. The games come in English, German, Spanish and French.

A floor plan drawn with the home icons opens in **3D**: look at it from above (drag or the arrow keys to turn, W A S D to move over it, the wheel to zoom), lift the roof off, or **Walk around** inside it — W A S D to walk, the arrow keys to look around, E or a click to open a door. It is drawn with WebGL where the browser has it: real depth, so nothing shows through a wall; the sun and its shadows; shingled roofs, wooden or tiled floors and painted walls; and outside, grass, trees, hills, a sky with clouds and a road along the front of the lot. Walls and furniture stop you rather than letting the view inside them. Rooms are named from what is in them, including rooms for more than one thing (a bed and a kitchen in one room is a **Studio**; also a great room, an eat-in kitchen and a living and dining room), and the **Closets** icons (a built-in closet, a closet rod, closet shelves and a bifold door) make a walk-in closet. The **Day** button goes round **Day**, **Evening** (a low gold sun and long shadows) and **Night** (stars and a moon, the lamps and the street lamps glowing, every window lit, and each room lit from inside when the roof is off), and **Save picture** keeps what the view shows as a PNG. **Settings** in the 3D view turns on and off a roof in one piece (hips and valleys where the parts of the house meet), gutters with downpipes and splash blocks, a light by every door out, a street out front (with a path from the front door and the drive carried across the pavement), the land and its size (its lines, corner stakes and the building line drawn on the ground, its sides measured, and its area, the house's footprint and floor area, and the yard in a corner), and the trees. It also sets the **Weather**: clear, cloudy, rain, a storm, snow or fog, with what is falling over the picture, wet ground or snow lying on the roof and the lawn, and soft shadows under cloud, plus how much water the roof sheds in a rainstorm or how much a snowfall on it weighs. Shadows stay sharp and sit where they are cast; walking inside, the sun comes in only through the windows and doors; and with the roof on, the rooms behind a window show their floors, walls, ceilings and furniture through the glass. A **Path light**, a **Porch light** and a **Floodlight** join the yard icons and light what is round them after dark. Doors sit in a frame, and walking is quicker (hold Shift to go twice as fast). A basement can be looked at on its own, the ground lifted off it.

**Start a house** (beside **Tidy up**, in a home design or an empty one) asks how many bedrooms and bathrooms, one floor or two, whether to have a basement, an open kitchen and dining room, an office, a laundry room, a garage and a walk-in closet, a roof in one piece, and whether the house stands on its own lot. It lays the house out the way houses are: a hall the width of the house, the bedrooms along one side of it (or upstairs) and the living room, kitchen and the rest along the other, so every room opens off the hall and the outside is a rectangle; the garage at the end of the front, through the laundry room; the stairs at the end of the hall, one flight over the other on every floor; and a family room, a utility room and storage in the basement. Then it furnishes it: a window in an outside wall of every room people live in, a front door, a garage door facing the street and a drive out to it. The rooms are either spread out on the paper and joined by arrows, or put together as they stand; in 3D a spread-out house comes together exactly as it was laid out. The result passes **Check** with nothing to put right, and one **Undo** takes it all away. **Floors and roof**, beside it, adds a floor above or a basement to a house drawn by hand (with stairs joined to the floor below and a hall round them) and turns the one-piece roof on or off.

Drawing by hand starts by asking what you are making. **Flowchart** gives the flowchart shapes, arrows and the Text and Code tabs; **Design** hides those and gives the icons instead (search with **/**, or pick a set), and each keeps its own paper and its own undo, so switching back and forth loses nothing. In a design, pick anything to see its size on the paper and in the panel: type a width, depth or height (6' 8", 2 m, 120 cm), or how high a picture hangs or a light drops from the ceiling. Things are kept to what holds them: a sofa can't grow wider than its room, a lamp than the table it stands on, and nothing goes through the ceiling; a room can't shrink smaller than what is in it. **Tidy up** squares a floor plan up: rooms meet at shared walls, furniture comes in out of the walls and apart, and doors and windows sit in their walls.

Something added from the panel goes where it would go in a home. A bed goes to a bedroom with its back to a wall, a stove joins the kitchen counters, and a nightstand goes by the head of the bed. A lamp goes on the nightstand, a coffee table in front of the sofa, a hood over the stove, and a TV on the wall the sofa faces. Nothing blocks a doorway, nothing tall stands in front of a window, and there is always room to walk in front of a bed or a counter. Where a room is too small, a sofa or a counter is made shorter to fit, and garden things go out in the yard. The plan shows every room's length, width and ceiling height under its square feet, and every piece's width, depth and height under its name (the **Sizes** switch, beside **Labels**). A picked piece has pills on the paper for its width, depth and height, to click and type into.

In 3D, the furniture is modeled: sofas of soft cushions on wooden legs, beds made up with pillows and a duvet, drawers with handles, basins and taps, lamps with lit shades, and plants in pots. Each piece is made of what it would be made of, so fabric has a weave, wood a grain, and steel and china shine. Pick a piece to choose its **Finish** (its main color and its trim). Pick a room for its **Materials**: the floor (wood boards, parquet, herringbone, tiles, hexagon tiles, checkered tiles, marble, slate, terrazzo, cork, carpet, concrete), the walls inside (paint, plaster, wallpaper, wood panels, shiplap, beadboard, tiles, brick, stone, concrete), and for the whole house the outside walls (siding, brick, stone, stucco, board and batten, cedar shakes, logs, cladding panels, corrugated metal, concrete) and the roof (shingles, clay tiles, metal, slate, thatch, wood shakes, a green roof, solar panels), each in the colors it comes in, or painted any color at all with the color ring at the end of its colors. The same choices are behind **Materials** in the 3D view, to change while you look. There are 40 more icons too, among them a chaise lounge, a wall oven, a corner bathtub, and new **Fitness & play** and **Garage & utility** sets (a treadmill, a pool table, a workbench, an EV charger), plus a fire pit, a gazebo, a garden shed and a swing set for the yard.

A floor plan can also be drawn spread out, the way one is first thought through: each room on its own, joined by the same arrows as a flowchart to the rooms it opens into, or to a door drawn between them. The arrows mostly come by themselves. A room added while another is picked is joined to it, a door put down near rooms is joined to them, and two rooms that met at a door and are pulled apart keep an arrow between them; pushed back together, the arrow turns into a door. In 3D the house puts itself together: each room slides up against the room it is joined to, with a door in the wall they share, and lies spread out again when it goes back to 2D. **Run** walks along the arrows, and **Check** counts an arrow as a way in. In a floor plan only rooms, doors, windows and stairs take arrows; circuits, networks, teams and a rocket's trip use them as before.

Circuits have more parts that work when the circuit runs: a single 1.5 V cell, a diode that lets current through one way only, a fuse that melts past its rating ("2 A") and opens the circuit, an ammeter and a voltmeter that say what they read, and a dimmer whose resistance is set by its words, like a resistor's. Space has comets and asteroids that go round the sun, a space station that circles the nearest planet, a lunar lander and a galaxy. Travel & city has a taxi, a streetcar, a helicopter and a scooter that drive their arrows like the other vehicles, plus an airport, a train station, a park and a café. Things & ideas has a gift, a target, an hourglass, music, a paint palette, a price tag, a magnet and a puzzle piece.

The **Trace table** after a run lists every step: the line and what it says, each name's new value (the main program's names first, then each module's under its name), and what was printed, with a small chart of every number over it.

A whole folder can be opened — even one of hundreds of thousands of files. The program is read from the file it starts in and the files that one uses; the 20 that matter most are tabs over the code, and every other file is in a list behind **+N more**, found by typing part of its name and brought in with a click. Code is only put right when you press **Build the chart**, never just by opening a file, and the button goes red and waits while it works.

To start over, **Settings → Clear everything…** clears the page for a clean slate: the pseudocode, the chart, the flowchart you drew, the code and its files, the run and the tests — and, unless you switch them off, saved progress and your colors and settings. It asks twice: what to clear, then whether to save a copy first, and it only goes ahead once you type **CLEAR**.

The three tabs are one program: start in any of them and edit in any other. Going to **Flowchart** turns the pseudocode's chart into a drawing you can change shape by shape; coming back writes the drawing's program into the pseudocode; **Code** is written from the pseudocode and read back into it. When the tab you go to has changes of its own, it asks first — save progress, keep that tab as it is, or replace it — and opening an example, a puzzle, a game, a file or saved progress asks the same way whenever there is work on the page.
